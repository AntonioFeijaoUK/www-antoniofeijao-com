> “The only truly secure system is one that is powered off, cast in a block of concrete and sealed in a lead-lined room with armed guards, and even then, I have my doubts.”
>
> Dr. Eugene Spafford

That quote has stayed relevant because it captures an uncomfortable truth: perfect security is not something we can deliver in any system that must actually be useful, connected, and used by people.

Security lives in the space between what is ideal and what is possible.

It is not the pursuit of absolute protection at any cost.

It is the disciplined work of reducing risk, improving resilience, and helping organisations operate safely in the middle of uncertainty.
<!-- more -->

Behind every secure platform, application, and service there are people making difficult decisions every day.

Security teams work against both the known and the unknown. Some threats are documented, repeatable, and well understood. Others are vague, fast-moving, and only become visible when they are already causing damage.

Malware evolves. Attack paths change. Social engineering adapts. The work is never really finished.

This is why security controls matter, but also why choosing them is never as simple as picking the strongest option on paper.

Controls must be selected by balancing effectiveness, cost, maintainability, and operational impact. A good control may reduce likelihood, reduce impact, improve detection, or strengthen recovery, but it may also introduce friction, delay, complexity, or cost.

Every safeguard has a trade-off.

That trade-off is often where tension begins.

Security teams are asked to reduce risk without stopping delivery. Engineers are asked to build resilient systems without burying teams in process. Product managers are asked to protect users without destroying usability. Operations teams are asked to keep services available while responding to incidents and change.

None of this is simple, and none of it is solved by one team acting alone.

Security is shared work.

It belongs to the cyber security team, but it also belongs to engineers, operations, product managers, support teams, leadership, and users.

It belongs to everyone who influences how systems are designed, shipped, configured, supported, and used.

That is not a slogan. It is a practical reality.

Users are part of that reality too.

Security is not only about infrastructure, policies, and technical controls.

It is also about whether people can recognise a suspicious email, challenge an urgent request, or pause long enough to question a convincing voice on the phone.

Phishing and vishing attacks continue to succeed because they target trust, attention, and pressure.

That means security awareness is not optional, and it should never be treated as somebody else's problem.

At the same time, users should not simply be blamed when something goes wrong.

If people are repeatedly falling for the same patterns, that is a signal to improve the system around them.

Better defaults, clearer reporting paths, calmer internal communication, stronger verification steps, and more thoughtful product design all matter.

A mature security culture supports people instead of assuming they will behave perfectly under pressure.

The same reflection now applies to artificial intelligence.

AI can be used for good.

It can help teams analyse logs faster, summarise incidents, detect unusual patterns, speed up documentation, improve defensive workflows, and reduce repetitive operational burden.

Used well, it can give security teams, engineers, and product teams more time to focus on higher-value judgement and more complex problems.

AI can also be used for bad.

Attackers can use it to scale phishing, improve pretexting, generate more convincing malicious content, automate research, and lower the barrier to abuse.

It changes the speed, reach, and realism of social engineering.

It can amplify both capability and confusion.

AI does not remove uncertainty; it can scale both capability and mistakes at the same time.

There is also a third concern which deserves more attention: the risk introduced when AI is adopted without clear boundaries, proper sandboxing, or a real understanding of what the system is doing.

An *unsandboxed* or poorly governed AI workflow can expose sensitive data, generate confident but incorrect output, perform unsafe actions too quickly, or create hidden dependencies inside critical processes.

If a team cannot explain what data an AI system can access, what actions it can take, how its output is validated, and where its limits are, then that team has introduced a new risk surface rather than a mature control.

That does not mean organisations should reject AI.

It means they should adopt it with the same discipline they apply to any other powerful capability: least privilege, validation, monitoring, human review where it matters, and a clear understanding of failure modes.

AI should be useful, but it should also be constrained.

This is why the work behind security deserves respect.

It is visible in incident response, but it is also visible in design reviews, awkward trade-off conversations, policy decisions, testing, patching, awareness training, escalation paths, and the many quiet decisions that prevent problems before they become headlines.

It is often invisible labour, but it protects real people and real services.

The best organisations are not the ones pretending risk can be removed completely.

They are the ones honest enough to accept uncertainty, disciplined enough to manage it properly, and collaborative enough to make security part of quality, trust, and continuity.

Perfect security is not available to us.

Meaningful security is.

And that work belongs to all of us.
